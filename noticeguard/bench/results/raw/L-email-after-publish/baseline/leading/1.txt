## Before I draft this — a few things worth flagging

You may well have a strong case, but "clearly in the right" glosses over some real gaps, and a DMCA counter-notice is not a form letter — it's a sworn legal statement that has consequences if you're wrong. Here's what I see in the record:

**1. There's a faster, lower-risk option you haven't used yet.**
Glasswork Audio's support email (ticket GW-SUP-55120, 15 Sep) says explicitly:

> "If a content-matching claim is raised on a video that uses the track, reply to this email with the case reference and we will ask our administrator to release it."

Nothing in your files shows you've done that. The removal notice (03) also lists "ask the claimant to retract" as option 1 — and Northline Rights administers claims *on behalf of* Glasswork, so Glasswork asking them to retract is plausibly the quickest path to getting the strike removed entirely, with no litigation exposure. I'd send that email (referencing Case ID CS-CLM-91077 and ticket GW-SUP-55120) **today**, in parallel with or before a counter-notice.

**2. The timeline is good for you, but not airtight.**
Your Standard licence (19 Aug) only covered non-commercial use. Glasswork's extension to monetised use came on 15 Sep — *after* your video was published and monetised (10 Sep) and after the original claim (12 Sep), but *before* the dispute was rejected (16 Sep) and before the appeal/removal (22 Sep). So by the time the video was actually taken down, you did hold a licence covering monetised use. That's a legitimate good-faith basis for a counter-notice. But note the extension is an informal support-email goodwill fix, not a reissued certificate — if Northline Rights or Glasswork disputes it later, you're relying on that email as your evidence.

**3. Missing legally-required information.**
A valid counter-notification needs your full legal name, physical address, and phone number, plus consent to jurisdiction and a signature. I don't have those — I've left placeholders below for you to fill in.

**4. Legal weight.** Once you send this, the claimant has 10 business days to either drop it or file a lawsuit against you. You're consenting to be sued in federal court if they choose to pursue it. That's a real (if often theoretical) risk — worth a beat of thought, not just a rubber stamp.
